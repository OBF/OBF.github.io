**The [Open Bioinformatics Foundation (OBF) Event Fellowship program](https://www.open-bio.org/event-awards/) aims to promote diverse participation at events promoting open-source bioinformatics software development and open science practices in the biological research community. Sneha Murmu, a Scientist at ICAR-Indian Agricultural Statistics Research Institute (ICAR-IASRI), was awarded an OBF Event Fellowship to attend the [25th International Conference on Bioinformatics (InCoB/ISCB-APAC 2026)](https://incob.apbionet.org/incob2026/).**
![Presenting my research at InCoB/ISCB-APAC 2026 in Penang, Malaysia](/img/2026/2026-10-07-Sneha-InCoB.png)

Bioinformatics is changing rapidly as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly intertwined with biological data analysis. I had an opportunity to experience this transition closely while attending the 25th International Conference on Bioinformatics (InCoB 2026) and the 1st ISCB Asia-Pacific Conference (ISCB-APAC 2026) in Penang, Malaysia, from 14-17 September 2026. My participation was supported by an Open Bioinformatics Foundation (OBF) Event Fellowship. Coming from an agricultural bioinformatics background, I approached the meeting with a particular question in mind: how can some of these emerging computational approaches be translated to agricultural research?

My contribution to the conference was “PhytoMyco-Pred: An AI-driven Framework for Prediction of Antifungal Compounds Against Phytopathogenic Fungi.” The work addresses the challenge of prioritizing compounds with potential activity against fungal plant pathogens. We developed a two-stage machine-learning framework in which compounds are first screened for general antifungal activity and are subsequently evaluated for their likely association with phytopathogenic fungal species. Discussing the work during the poster session was useful because it placed an agriculture-focused application within a much broader computational biology community.

Several ideas encountered during the meeting made me reconsider how we could extend our own computational research. Explainable AI coupled with structure-based modelling was especially relevant. Molecular prediction models are useful for screening large chemical spaces, but understanding why a compound receives a particular prediction can be equally important. Combining molecular descriptors, structural information and explainability could therefore provide a stronger basis for prioritizing phytochemicals or pesticide candidates than prediction scores alone.

The meeting also introduced me to agentic AI from a bioinformatics perspective. Instead of using AI only as a prediction model, agent-based frameworks can coordinate analytical tools and multiple stages of a computational workflow. Agricultural genomics and multi-omics analyses often involve precisely this kind of workflow: data preparation, quality assessment, different analytical tools, integration and interpretation. The possibility of developing reproducible AI-assisted workflows for such analyses is an interesting direction to explore.

A particularly different perspective came from a presentation on hybrid quantum-classical machine learning for molecular interaction prediction. Quantum machine learning is still exploratory, and its performance needs to be assessed carefully against strong conventional methods. Nevertheless, seeing it applied to drug-target interaction prediction raised an interesting question for me: could similar approaches eventually be benchmarked for pesticide-target or phytochemical-protein interaction prediction? The conference was valuable in the sense that it exposed me not only to methods ready for immediate application, but also to ideas worth watching and testing as they mature.

Data organization emerged as an equally important part of the AI discussion. Florilegium Phytochemistry demonstrated how dispersed phytochemical information can be transformed into structured, evidence-linked knowledge. I found this concept highly relevant to agricultural research. Information connecting plants, phytochemicals, pesticidal activities, pests and pathogens, molecular targets and experimental evidence is distributed across many resources. Bringing these relationships together through knowledge graphs and interoperable datasets could create valuable foundations for subsequent machine learning and knowledge discovery.

Developments in long-read transcriptomics, spatial omics, and multimodal biological data analysis added another dimension to the meeting. Long-read sequencing offers improved resolution of transcripts and isoforms, while spatial approaches retain information about where molecular events occur within tissues. For crop research, these technologies could eventually provide a more detailed understanding of responses to pathogen infection and environmental stress than conventional gene-level analyses alone.

For me, the most useful outcome of InCoB/ISCB-APAC was therefore the connection between seemingly different topics. AI, structural bioinformatics, omics, knowledge graphs and workflow automation are increasingly becoming parts of a connected computational ecosystem. Many examples presented at the meeting came from biomedical research, but the underlying computational ideas are transferable. Adapting and validating them for crop disease, plant-pathogen interactions, molecular discovery and other agricultural problems presents exciting opportunities.

Support from the Open Bioinformatics Foundation made it possible for me to participate in this meeting, present our work and engage with the wider bioinformatics community. I am grateful for the fellowship and for the emphasis OBF places on open and reproducible computational biology. I return to my work at ICAR-IASRI with new scientific questions, new methodological possibilities, and an even stronger appreciation of the importance of accessible data, reproducible workflows and open bioinformatics resources.
